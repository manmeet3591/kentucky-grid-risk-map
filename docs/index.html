      What this map shows

      A screening view for Kentucky utilities and emergency managers, built from open data. It answers two questions: which transmission lines run through the most trees, and trees tall enough to fall onto them (more tree contact means more outage risk in wind, ice and storms), and which critical facilities had flood water at or near them when Sentinel-1 radar passed over during the February and April 2025 floods.

      1. Vegetation along transmission lines

      • Lines: HIFLD Open Electric Power Transmission Lines (138, 161, 345, 500 and 765 kV in Kentucky; overhead only). HIFLD has no 69 kV lines in Kentucky, so 69 kV lines come from OpenStreetMap (power=line, voltage=69000). Ways shared with a higher-voltage circuit are left out because HIFLD already covers them.
      • Canopy: USFS / NLCD Tree Canopy Cover , from the MRLC web coverage service on its native Albers grid.
      • Each line is clipped to Kentucky and cut into ~1 km segments. Each segment gets a m buffer either side (~90 m corridor: a typical right-of-way plus a fall-in margin for trees at its edge).
      • Score = mean canopy cover (%) of the 30 m cells in the corridor. We also report the share of cells with ≥ 50 % canopy. County values are length-weighted means over the line segments whose midpoint lies in the county. "km in heavy canopy" counts segments with mean canopy ≥ 40 %.
      • Tree height: Meta / World Resources Institute global canopy height map v1 (Tolan et al. 2024, 1 m, from Maxar satellite imagery; AWS Open Data s3://dataforgood-fb-data/forests/v1/). Imagery dates behind Kentucky's line corridors: . Only the corridors are kept; pixels under the product's cloud mask are dropped, and segments where over half the corridor is masked show "no data".
      • Tree height p95 = 95th percentile height of the tree pixels (≥ 3 m) in the corridor. It describes the tall trees present, not how many there are: a segment with a single tree row can score high. Segments with almost no trees (< 100 m² of tree pixels) get 0.
      • Fall-in reach = share of the segment's length (in 10 m steps along the line) where, on either side, at least 5 m² of trees are taller than their horizontal distance to the line centreline, i.e. could reach the centreline if they fell straight towards it. County values are km-weighted; "fall-in km" = Σ segment length × share. Segments with ≥ 25 % fall-in count as exposed.

      2. Critical facilities in flood water

      • Facilities: OpenStreetMap via Overpass. Gas stations (amenity=fuel), hospitals, substations (power=substation), water and wastewater plants, and fuel terminals (oil/fuel industrial sites and fuel storage tanks). Tanks within 250 m of each other are merged into one terminal site.
      • Water: NASA/JPL OPERA DSWx-S1 (Dynamic Surface Water Extent from Sentinel-1 radar, 30 m). Classes: open water, inundated vegetation, not water, HAND-masked, layover/shadow.
      • For each event, every Sentinel-1 pass in the event window is compared with passes from two dry seasons (). Flood water = water on any event pass where no reference pass saw water. Water seen in both is normal water (rivers, lakes, ponds).
      • Facility status uses the grid cells around the OSM point (cells ~25 × 31 m):
        • Flooded: ≥ 2 flood-water cells within the site window (3 × 3 cells ≈ 75 × 95 m; 5 × 5 for water plants and fuel terminals).
        • Near flood water: not flooded, but flood water within ~110 m of the site window.
        • Normal water edge: water at the site in the dry-season passes too (river intakes, lagoons), so it isn't counted as flooded.
        • Dry: seen as not-water on at least one event pass. Not observed: no event pass covered the site (or only terrain-masked / radar-shadow pixels).

      Limitations and data gaps

      • Revisit gaps. In 2025 Sentinel-1 passed over any one spot in Kentucky only every few days (Sentinel-1A alone until Sentinel-1C entered service), so floods that peaked and receded between passes are missed. In February 2025 no pass with data covered a north–south strip of eastern Kentucky: 19 counties from Mason, Lewis and Fleming south through Morgan, Breathitt, Perry, Knott, Leslie, Letcher and Harlan. That strip includes much of the 15–16 Feb flash flooding. Pike County was covered on 17 and 19 Feb, after the peak. Those sites show as "not observed", not as dry. Flash floods in narrow Appalachian valleys are also often narrower than the 30 m cells.
      • Radar confusions. Smooth surfaces (parking lots, flat roofs, tank tops, wet snow, which fell on 19–20 Feb) can look like water. Flooding under dense trees or between buildings is often missed. Wind-roughened ponds can look dry. Treatment-plant basins and lagoons can show as "flood water" when the dry-season passes happened to miss them. Check flagged sites against local reports.
      • Pass timing. A facility marked dry was dry on the passes listed, not necessarily at the flood peak.
      • OSM coverage. OSM facility mapping is incomplete and uneven (e.g. many rural gas stations, small substations and water plants are missing or mapped only as points). Points are site centroids, not footprints. Utility and KYEM asset lists would improve this.
      • Line data. HIFLD geometry is generalised and may be several years old. OSM 69 kV coverage is incomplete (and HIFLD has none). Underground lines are excluded. Distribution lines, where most tree-caused outages happen, aren't included.
      • Canopy vintage. Canopy is 2021 data. Right-of-way clearing since then, storm damage and new growth aren't reflected, and 30 m cells blur the edge of a cleared corridor. Species and health matter as much as cover and height and aren't in this data.
      • Tree height accuracy and vintage. The 1 m height map is a model estimate from single satellite images, not lidar. Its published mean absolute error is roughly 3 m against airborne lidar, larger for tall closed forest (heights tend to be underestimated) and at the edges of crowns and clearings. Heights are integer metres. The imagery is mostly , older than the 2021 canopy data, so right-of-way clearing, storm damage and growth since then are not reflected.
      • Fall-in is a geometric screen. It measures to the line centreline, ignores conductor height and sag (a tree must be taller than √(distance² + conductor height²) to touch a wire), phase spacing on wide lines, slope, lean and wind direction. Line positions from HIFLD/OSM can be off by tens of metres, which moves trees in and out of reach. Treat it as a relative ranking, not a list of trees to cut.
      • This is a screening tool, not an engineering or vegetation-management assessment.

      Data sources

      • Transmission lines: HIFLD Open Electric Power Transmission Lines (public domain); OpenStreetMap contributors (ODbL).
      • Tree canopy: USFS / NLCD Tree Canopy Cover, CONUS 2021 (public domain).
      • Tree height: Meta / WRI High Resolution Canopy Height Maps (CC BY 4.0). Tolan, J. et al. (2024), Very high resolution canopy height maps from RGB imagery using self-supervised vision transformer and convolutional decoder trained on aerial lidar, Remote Sensing of Environment 300, 113888.
      • Flood water: NASA OPERA DSWx-S1 (PO.DAAC OPERA_L3_DSWX-S1_V1), served via NASA GIBS; contains modified Copernicus Sentinel-1 data.
      • Facilities: OpenStreetMap via Overpass API (ODbL). Counties: US Census TIGERweb.
